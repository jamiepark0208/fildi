## 1st-level analysis

On SPY, closing short puts at 50% of max profit beats holding to expiry by 56% in dollars over 22 years — but the edge is one crash dodge, not a broad pattern. Losers are rare (8 of 222) but ~7× the average win (avg loss −$689 vs avg win +$100; worst −$2,373, Oct 2008). Annualized, the 50% rule is only ~2.0%/yr on strike collateral, and hold-to-expiry scored slightly higher on that metric (56.3% chained vs 54.3%) with fewer trades.

## 2nd-level analysis

Jamie's book is not SPY. His names gap 20%+ overnight (Fri Nov 6: IREN +20%, OKLO +22.7% in a day; Thu Nov 5: semis −11% to −22% in a session). The March-2020-style dodge that drives the SPY result may not repeat on single names — and the 50% rule's losers stay open to expiry with no stop, which is exactly the wrong shape for names that can gap through a strike in one print. Importing the rule as-is would be trusting a one-trade edge on the wrong universe. The honest next step is not adoption but a test on his own fills: if 50% still wins ex-the-worst-trade on his book, adopt it; if not, hold stands and the SPY result is filed as a non-transferable artifact.

## Use cases

1. **Jamie's next short put: close at 50% or hold?** The study answers with his own fills, not SPY's. Why: the exit rule is the highest-frequency decision in the book — every position faces it.
2. **Fildi exit default.** If 50% wins ex-worst-trade on dollars AND drawdown, set it as the suggested exit in the options view; if not, keep hold-to-expiry and say so in the file. Why: a default that is wrong costs a little on every trade.
3. **Sizing.** The study's avg-win / avg-loss / worst-trade per name feeds max-loss sizing (A365's strike minimums use the same tail numbers). Why: the 50% rule's losers are ~7× wins on SPY — the tail, not the win rate, sets the size.
